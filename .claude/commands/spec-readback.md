# /spec-readback — read the model back to a person

The person never reads the Lean. The readback is the model in a form a person can take in and correct, generated by the checker from the model itself, so it cannot say something the model does not. It is cut two ways, and both are valid:

- per area: `out/readback/<area>.html`, the area as it stands, with `out/readback/index.html` listing the areas;
- per change: `out/readback/change.html`, every area the change touches and what the change did in each. This is the one to read for a change, above all one that crosses areas.

An area's page holds:

- the journeys: each business case in general terms, as a graph of its usual course, with a loop on a step that can repeat and, beside every step, each way the system turns it down; they come in the order of the area's flow, the journeys about one thing together under its name;
- under each journey its walkthroughs: specific runs worth knowing, moment by moment, told in the area's named views;
- the assumptions: what the model takes for granted, simplifies or leaves out;
- for an area extracted from code, what the code offers: every way into it, and whether the model covers it, with the count of what is left out;
- for an area with parts, the ways in that several parts speak about: which part owns each, which take it as given, and any that two parts both model;
- every invariant and requirement, in plain words, with how strongly it is verified and where it comes from;
- the state machines: one diagram per kind of thing that has a life of its own;
- the open questions that are the person's to answer;
- the decisions already taken, with their reasons;
- for an area bound to code: whether the code agrees, and what is compared.

1. Generate it. Run `lake exe check`: it writes the page of every area. When the work is a change in git and the branch has a base (`git merge-base HEAD origin/main`, with the repository's default branch), run `lake exe check --since <that revision>` instead: it also writes the page of the change, and each area's page can then be narrowed to what the change did. Without git, or for a first version, there are only the area pages.
2. Before handing it over, read the area as the person will: is every bound of the scope, every stand-in for the outside world, and everything left out said in `assumptions`, in plain words? Is every journey moment told in views the person knows? Fix what is not (through `/spec`) and generate again.
3. Tell the person which page to open (the change's page for a change, the area's page otherwise) and, in a few plain sentences and in this order:
   - the journeys, first: ask whether these are the business cases, whether each usual course is what really happens, and whether any way of being turned down is missing or wrong; then the walkthroughs;
   - the assumptions: ask whether any of them makes the model too far from the real thing;
   - anything violated, failed, or drifting between code and model;
   - for a change: what it does to the requirements (new, removed, reworded, verified more weakly than before);
   - claims below their target, and why; claims you proposed yourself, which need a yes or a no;
   - the open questions that are theirs to answer;
   - for an area extracted from code: that the binding under "what is compared" needs their confirmation.
4. Ask for corrections and answers. Each goes back through `/spec`, with the person and the date added to the `sources` of what it changes; then run this command again.

You do not record approval. A change is approved when its pull request is approved, with the committed ledger as the record of what was verified.
